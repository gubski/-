AI Biases in Healthcare

A Research Proposal on Why They Occur, Their Social Impact, and How to Fix Them

Study Methodology Proseminar | MIT Research Proposal Format
Section 1 / Introduction

What is the Core Research Question?

We often assume computers are completely neutral and objective. However, artificial intelligence in medicine frequently treats different patient groups unequally (Cross et al., 2024).

  • The Core Question: How and why do biases emerge in healthcare AI, and what are their consequences for societal health equity?
  • Why It Matters: As clinics increasingly rely on automated tools for diagnosis and triage, hidden biases can actively harm vulnerable groups and worsen existing social inequalities (Abu-Mahfouz, 2026).
Section 2 / Literature Review

What Do Previous Studies Tell Us?

Recent academic literature shows that bias is not an isolated technical accident. Instead, it is built directly into the research and data pipeline (Cross et al., 2024).

  • Cross et al. (2024): Demonstrates that bias compounds across every stage of development—from data collection and labeling to real-world hospital deployment.
  • Abu-Mahfouz (2026): Highlights that without active statistical corrections, AI systems reinforce historical prejudices under the illusion of mathematical fairness.
Section 3 / Methodology

How Do We Study This Problem?

Following a standard research proposal layout, our investigation analyzes how data is gathered and processed before models are built.

Data Source Evaluation

Inspecting Electronic Health Records (EHRs) to see which patient demographics are missing or underrepresented (Cross et al., 2024).

Pipeline Auditing

Tracing how input variables, human labels, and performance targets interact to create unfair outputs (Abu-Mahfouz, 2026).

Section 4 / Mechanism Analysis

Why Do These Biases Occur?

  • Skewed Data Collection: Training sets often lack data from minority or low-income groups, making the AI less accurate for them (Abu-Mahfouz, 2026).
  • The Proxy Variable Trap: Using past healthcare spending as a shortcut for "medical need." Because disadvantaged groups historically spent less due to poor access, the AI learns to ignore their actual sickness (Cross et al., 2024).
  • Human Annotator Bias: AI learns from human doctors. If doctors harbor unconscious prejudices, the AI copies and scales those exact flaws (Cross et al., 2024).
Section 5 / Impact Assessment

What Is the Impact on Society?

  • Widening Health Disparities: Biased software delays treatments and misallocates resources, hurting minority and low-income populations the most (Abu-Mahfouz, 2026).
  • Automation Bias: Busy doctors might trust computer outputs too blindly, leading to human errors and a loss of personal clinical judgment (Cross et al., 2024).
  • Erosion of Trust: When communities realize automated triage systems treat them unfairly, overall trust in medical science breaks down.
Section 6 / Mitigation Strategy

How Do We Fix the Problem?

Data-Level Fixes

Deliberately balance training data sets, include social determinants of health (SDOH), and apply re-weighting techniques (Abu-Mahfouz, 2026).

Algorithmic Safeguards

Use "explainable AI" (XAI) so doctors can see *why* a computer made a choice, and run strict fairness audits before hospital launch (Cross et al., 2024).

Section 7 / Conclusion & Contribution

What Does This Proposal Achieve?

This research framework moves past simple complaints about technology and offers a structured path forward.

  • Clear Diagnosis: Proves that bias is a systemic design flaw, not a random glitch.
  • Actionable Governance: Emphasizes that technical tools must be paired with strict regulatory oversight and ethical audits.
  • Equitable Future: Ensures that future medical AI benefits all patient populations equally (Cross et al., 2024).
